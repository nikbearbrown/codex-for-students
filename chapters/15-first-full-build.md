## The project brief

A Codex project at student-scale that exercises all five supervisory capacities.

The chapter does not prescribe a specific project; the project is yours. The shape:

- **Multi-step.** At least five Code Mode prompts. Below this, the discipline is overhead.
- **Has aesthetic dimensions.** Either visual (a small web app, a data visualization) or stylistic (a tool whose output another human reads — formatting, voice, structure all matter).
- **Touches your real life.** Something you would actually use or share. A personal automation, a small portfolio piece, a tool for a club or class.
- **Scope-appropriate.** Doable in a 3–5 hour single sitting or across two evenings. Not a semester project. Not a one-prompt exercise.

Candidate projects from past students:

- **A personal-finance summary tool** (Seth's example from the book; processes bank CSVs into monthly summaries).
- **A reading-list manager** (tracks what you have read, what you want to read, and books friends recommended; outputs sortable lists).
- **A study-tracker for a single class** (logs your study sessions, identifies topics where you have spent the least time, recommends what to study next).
- **A small portfolio website** (per Chapter 10's Brutalist three-file system; one page per project you have built).
- **A homework-summary script** (per Chapter 7's running example; reads your calendar and produces a morning summary of imminent assignments).
- **A vocab study tool** (for a class learning a non-English language; spaced repetition against a word list you maintain).

Pick one. Or pick something else that fits the shape. The brief is the orientation; the choice is yours.

## The complete sequence

The book in fourteen steps. Apply each to your project.

1. **Ask Mode interrogation** (Chapter 7).
2. **One-sentence problem formulation** (Chapter 7).
3. **Minimum viable spec** (Chapter 7).
4. **AGENTS.md** (Chapter 6).
5. **DESIGN.md and PROJECT.md** if the project is creative (Chapter 10).
6. **Ask Mode plan** (Chapter 11).
7. **Plan review** (Chapter 11).
8. **First Code Mode prompt** with five-element specification (Chapter 8).
9. **Read the output; predict before reading** (Chapter 5's PA).
10. **Handoff condition check** (Chapter 9).
11. **Plausibility audit on the result** (Chapter 5's PA).
12. **Update AGENTS.md** with lessons learned (Chapter 6).
13. **After all steps: three-pass verification** (Chapter 13).
14. **Post-build learning document** (Chapter 13).

Steps 8–12 repeat per step in the build. Steps 1–7 happen once, upstream. Steps 13–14 happen once, at the end.

## What success looks like

Not a perfect build.

A build where:

- You can **account for every Codex prompt** that ran. What it asked for, why you asked it, what alternative you considered, what you would change.
- The **AGENTS.md grew** with lessons learned from this build. Even one entry counts.
- The **post-build document** contains a specific "what I would do differently" that names a real decision you would reverse.
- The supervisory work was **labeled** in the build log — at least the major capacity moments (PA catches, PF revisits, EI integration checks).
- The **project works** for the use you built it for.

A build that meets these criteria is the chapter's success. Whether it took three hours or six is less important than whether it taught you what the discipline costs and what it provides.

---

## What it will probably *not* be like

A few things to expect.

**The first attempt will produce at least one failure the discipline catches.** A handoff condition that fails on a first run. A plausibility audit that fires on something the spec did not anticipate. A revert-and-respecify on a step where the second correction did not work. These are not signs of a failed build; they are signs of the discipline working.

**The build will take longer than you estimate.** The first conducted build always does. By the third, the discipline is reflex.

**You will be tempted to skip the post-build document.** "I'll write it tomorrow." You will not. Write it now, at the end of the build, before the lessons fade. Thirty minutes. One page. The artifact pays back.

**The AGENTS.md you write will be revised twice during the build.** The first version will be incomplete; the build will surface things you did not know to write down. This is fine. Revise as you go.

## What would change my mind

The chapter's claim is that **completing the conducted build produces a step-change in supervisory capacity** — that the practitioner who has built one full project with the discipline is materially more reliable on subsequent Codex use than the practitioner who has only read about the discipline. If a controlled comparison found no measurable difference between students who completed Chapter 14 and students who completed Chapters 0–13 without the capstone, the chapter becomes formative rather than load-bearing.

I expect the difference to be substantial because the capstone is where the framework becomes practice. Reading produces the framework; building consolidates it.

## Still puzzling

- **The right scope for the capstone build.** The book describes a shape; the specifics are yours. Where the right size is varies by reader.

- **Whether the post-build document should be shared.** Sharing produces accountability for honesty. It also produces the incentive to perform. Candid private, redacted public.

- **What comes after the chapter.** Most readers will do a second build, then a third, and the discipline will become reflex over months. Some will return to *Claude Code for Students* or *GitHub Copilot CLI for Students* to learn the variant disciplines. Some will reach for the teachers companion to start designing classroom activities. The book ends; the practice continues.

## AI Wayback Machine

🕰️ **John Dewey** (1859–1952) — philosopher of education whose *Experience and Education* (1938) argued that learning is *the transformation of the learner through purposeful experience*, not the deposit of information into the learner.[^1] Dewey's claim was that the cognitive structures that constitute durable learning are built through *engaged practice* on real tasks, not through reading-about-practice or being-told-about-practice. The post-build learning document is Dewey applied to AI-assisted coding: the record that the experience changed the person, not just the codebase. The full conducted build is the experience. The document is the record of what the experience built. Dewey was writing about classroom learning; the form scales to the practitioner's first capstone build.
